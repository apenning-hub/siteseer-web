Ask any construction educator what students learn best from, and you'll hear the same answer: being on site. Standing under a half-built roof, watching a slab cure, tracing a service run through a wall cavity before it's closed up — these are the moments where theory snaps into place.

The problem is that those moments are getting harder to arrange.

## Why the site visit broke

A live construction site is a working business with a schedule, a safety regime and a liability profile. Bringing a cohort of twenty or thirty students onto it means inductions, PPE, supervision ratios, insurance, and a builder willing to pause productive work. Multiply that by every stage of construction a student should see — excavation, footings, frame, lock-up, fit-out — and the logistics collapse.

So most programs ration site access. A single visit, late in the build, when it's safe and convenient. Students see the *result* of a process they never watched unfold.

> You can't teach a building from a textbook — but you increasingly can't teach it from a site visit either.

## The cost is hidden, then sudden

The cost doesn't show up in a single semester. It shows up years later, in graduates who can recite the sequence of construction but have never *seen* it; who know what a bearer is but couldn't point to one mid-frame. Employers absorb the gap as slower onboarding and more supervision — and, in a tight labour market, as roles left unfilled.

## Removing the bottleneck

The answer isn't to abandon real site visits — it's to stop depending on them for everything. If students can walk an accurate, explorable model of a build *at every stage*, on the laptop they already own, the single real visit becomes the capstone rather than the whole experience.

That's the gap SiteSeer was built to close: not a replacement for the site, but a way to make the site visitable, repeatably, by everyone.
